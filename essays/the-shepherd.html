The Shepherd

November 2026

There was once a boy tasked with tending the town’s sheep who flippantly decided to yell “Wolf” when none threatened, deceiving the townsfolk who ran to the rescue of their flock. Upon finding the boy laughing at them for falling for his ruse, the townsfolk warned him of the perils of dishonesty. The boy repeated his lie, each time earning the ire of his neighbors. Lo and behold, in time a wolf appeared, as wolves tend to do; the boy called for help, none came, and the wolf decimated the flock.

The moral is frequently written that liars are not to be believed, even when telling the truth. Deeper, and more pertinent to any free people, is this: when a town places a proven liar in a position to tend its wellbeing and warn it of danger, it forfeits its own wellbeing and safety. And the reverse holds just as surely: a town whose people speak the truth to one another keeps its flock.

First, liars are not to be believed, even when telling the truth. A three-year-old knows she is expected to tell the truth, and that in her family’s compact there is a penalty for lying. An employee found to be consistently dishonest is rightfully fired. A witness who lies under oath faces jail. That we should ever need to reestablish the expectation that a liar be chastised by his community illuminates the rot in our relationships with one another. Upon being proven, beyond any reasonable doubt, to be a bald-faced liar, one should lose the standing to participate in our collective governance. We need not dwell on the small lies, which stem from the pettiness of an unserious person. It is the big lies, about where one places his ultimate allegiances, about what he hides, about his commitment to the rights and laws that bind us, that disqualify.

Second, we must not follow the liar down the dishonest path he calls us toward. As we are responsible for our own behavior, we must not accept as normal the example that we can stare each other in the eye and state falsehood as fact. The liar tempts us to believe that dishonesty will enable us, that it will let us do greater things than we can achieve through honesty, and that through dishonesty we will make ourselves great. Our personal virtue requires that we stand resolutely on honesty, within our own behavior, and as importantly, that we demand the same from those who inform us and represent us.

Third, a shepherd’s warning is only as good as his honesty. The danger a leader is tasked with warning us of is not the type we should take lightly; it is the ominous danger that stands to unravel our safety and our basic ability to govern ourselves. When a known liar tells us that public money is not being deposited into his own accounts, will we believe him? When he tells us that we must attack another country, will we believe him? When he tells us that he must restrict our rights to free speech, a free press, or the freedom of assembly, all for our own safety, will we believe him? If we cannot answer yes to these, the gravest questions we bear as citizens, then we must soberly reckon with the peril we have chosen.

Fourth, a community that speaks the truth to one another thrives. Within that somber reflection there is a spirit that calls us to something better than the unreasoning dishonesty we are fed. If we chose our way into this hazard, we can choose our way out. A town whose people tell each other the truth does not depend on a single shepherd to sound the alarm; every neighbor becomes a watchman, and every warning can be trusted. Our daily actions can cast ballots of censure against dishonesty, and our demands for vigilance from those who represent us can supply what a dishonest leader cannot.

When a town allows a liar to lead, the liar casts a wide net of uncertainty, the liar fosters an inability to distinguish danger, and when the wolf appears, as wolves tend to do, ruin must follow. But when a town holds fast to the truth, and to one another, the flock is kept and the community thrives.
