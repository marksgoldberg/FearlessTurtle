To Inure or Not to Inure

On honesty, and why a free people must never accept dishonesty as normal · November 2026

A public figure who builds his standing on dishonesty razes the standards of basic honesty for everyone around him. He mischaracterizes those who differ from him, he mischaracterizes every opponent he faces, and he mischaracterizes the numbers of people who actually support him, until his place at the forefront of public life rests squarely on falsehood. When such a figure rises, the evidence of his lies fills volumes, and as surely as the sun rises each day, his next public statement will confirm the pattern. However, cataloguing and condemning any one person’s lies is not the purpose of this essay. The purpose rather is to call forth a response from a free people that rejects any public behavior, from any person, of any party or persuasion, that shames us with blatant dishonesty.

The liar in public life lies under the protection of the very freedoms he abuses, for a free society guarantees our right to free speech rather than our collective right to honest speech. Indeed, history consistently shows that the right to free speech opens the door to shameful and dishonest political speech, and that in human nature lies a true talent for misleading one another, most notably when power is on the line. That is to say, the dishonest public figure is nothing new.

However, a free society permits these elements of decay into public discourse certainly not because public dishonesty is good, but rather because inherent in self-government lies the call to refute nonsense with reason and logic, to refute lies with verifiable fact, and to refute the purveyor of lies with the basic human call to decency. Said differently, while the tree of liberty cannot grow in soil consisting solely of blatant dishonesty, the refutation of dishonesty enriches that soil with the very nourishment that feeds the tree of liberty. Liberty does not exist in anarchy, it does not exist in dictatorship, and it does not exist in demagoguery, for liberty cannot exist when a citizenry cannot trust the words of its leaders.

In addition to liberty, fundamental law and order is predicated upon basic honesty. It is no exaggeration to say that a system of law and order would cease to exist without it. In any just court, the weighing of evidence by rationally minded judges and jurors, who rely on the oath of fellow citizens at the pitch of critical moments to tell the whole truth and nothing but the truth, is the very means to justice. In fact, a violation of that oath is punishable with jail time, as civic society acknowledges that there must be repercussions when one bears a burden to tell the truth and fails to live up to it.

When it comes to our leaders, it is self-evident that a leader’s blatant lying to the people fundamentally fails to make a nation great. Nor is a leader’s mere basic honesty enough to make a nation great. The type of greatness a free people is capable of, and that we all desire, requires far more from us and from those who represent us than basic honesty and simple reason, for true greatness rests on traits of character far more noble than basic honesty. But if our leaders dismiss honesty with a shrug, and if we follow suit, any claim to greatness is dismissed in turn.

As we find it essential to hold our children to telling the truth, so we must hold those who lead us to the same minimum standard. Basic honesty is not noble, and it is not the ultimate outcome we should strive for; it is the starting point from which we take on our more expansive challenges. As individuals, we choose how we behave and how we hold our family and friends to account for theirs. As citizens, we hold this same sacred honor over our representatives. For while they are asked to represent us, they bear the sacred obligation to state basic facts as fact. And when they stray from basic honesty and decency, when they renounce their part in this compact, they must feel the full weight of our reprehension.

The greatest danger is not the liar himself, for there have always been liars. The greatest danger is that we grow used to him: that the shock fades, that the outrage dulls, and that what was once indecent comes to seem merely ordinary. To inure ourselves to dishonesty is to surrender, a little at a time and without ever deciding to, the very ground on which a free people stands. So the choice is ours, and it is made daily. We must not inure.
