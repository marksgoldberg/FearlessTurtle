The Empty Stool

November 2026

There was once a diner off the plaza of an old desert town where a single coffee cup hung on a hook behind the counter, apart from all the others. It belonged to a regular, a quiet man who had come in every night for years until, one season, he did not. There had been some trouble. Rumors went around the town the way rumors do, each one more confident than the last. The owner listened to none of them. When customers asked whose cup it was, he said only that it belonged to a friend who was running late. He said it for years.

We are often told that loyalty is a virtue, and so it is. Deeper, and more difficult, is this: loyalty is not believing the best about someone no matter what; it is refusing to believe the worst about him on the strength of gossip alone. It is to keep his place until the truth is known, and to tell him the truth yourself when it is.

First, rumor is the cheapest currency a town has. It costs nothing to repeat and it buys a great deal: a moment of importance, the pleasure of being in the know, the comfort of seeing someone else brought low. In my years reporting on the police I learned how rarely the first story was the true one, and how impossible it was to call the first story back once it had gone around. A decent person treats rumor the way a careful man treats a counterfeit bill. He does not pass it along simply because someone handed it to him.

Second, loyalty is not blindness. The truest friends I had in Santa Fe were not the ones who told me I was right. They were the ones who told me, plainly and to my face, when I was wrong: when I drank too much, when I had grown hard, when a story I was chasing was costing more than it was worth. The friend who will only flatter you is not loyal to you; he is loyal to his own comfort. Keeping faith with someone means keeping faith with the truth about him, and saying it to him rather than about him.

Third, loyalty must never become complicity. There is a counterfeit loyalty that protects a friend from the consequences of real wrongdoing, that hides the bruise, buries the ledger and closes ranks. Every town has its brotherhoods of that kind, and they do enormous harm while calling themselves faithful. The test is simple. True loyalty will stand beside a friend while the facts are found, and it will accept what the facts say. False loyalty will do anything to keep the facts from being found at all.

Fourth, keeping faith is keeping a place. Most of what loyalty asks of us is not dramatic. It is the cup on the hook, the stool held open, the letter answered, the name spoken kindly in a room where it is being spoken unkindly. These are small things. They cost little, and they are worth a great deal to the person who learns of them later, as people usually do.

When a town lets rumor do its judging, it convicts the innocent along with the guilty and learns to trust no one. But when a few of its people hold a place open and wait for the truth, they keep something alive in that town that is worth more than any single reputation: the belief that a person is more than the worst thing said about him.
