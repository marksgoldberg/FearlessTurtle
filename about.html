About Nathaniel Greyback

Most biographies begin with a date and a place, and this one will too. But the dates did not mean much to Nathaniel Greyback. He measured his life the way the sea measures things. He measured it in tides and in seasons and in the slow arrival of what was always going to come. What follows is what can be put together from the few papers that survive, from the people who drank with him, and from the long gaps in between. The gaps are where he did most of his living.

The Eastern Shore

He was born in 1853 on the eastern beaches of South Africa. The sand there is warm and the Indian Ocean comes in heavy and green. He was a loggerhead, as his mother had been and her mother before her. He came into the world the way loggerheads do. He came in the dark, in a crowd of brothers and sisters, and they all ran down the beach in the moonlight toward water they had never seen and already understood.

Most of them did not make it. The ghost crabs took some and the gulls took some and the sea took the rest. He made it. Years later, in a bar, a man was rude enough to ask him about it. He said he had been lucky, and that luck was the first thing a turtle learns not to trust. Then he finished his drink. He did not say anything more about it, and the man did not ask again.

What he took from that beach was not sadness. It was a habit of paying attention. He learned to read the world by reading the water. He learned which way it pulled and how warm it ran and what it brought in and what it carried off. Later he said it was the only thing he ever really knew how to do. He did it as a traveler and he did it as a reporter and he did it as a writer, and he said it was the same work each time, only pointed at different things.

The Long Drift

For many years he traveled the sea currents of the world. Nobody knows how many years, and he was no help. He rode the Agulhas down past the Cape. He let the great gyres take him where they wanted to. He followed the warm water east and the cold water north. He saw Madagascar and Mozambique and the long empty beaches of Western Australia. He saw the islands of the South Pacific, and Chile, and Peru. He saw a thousand places with no names he could say, and some with too many names to choose from.

It was hard and it was good. The water was cold at night and the sun was hard in the day, and there were sharks, and there was the loneliness, which was worse than the sharks. But there was the morning when the light came up gray and then gold over the swells, and there were the flying fish, and there was the feeling of the current under him, steady and strong, taking him somewhere. He said that a man who has not been alone a long time on open water does not know what he is made of. He said it is not a bad thing to find out, but you should not do it on purpose.

He met many people, and he met them on their terms. He fished with the fishermen and ate what they gave him. He learned a little of many languages and a lot of none. He sat at fires on beaches where no one had ever seen anything like him, and nobody seemed very troubled by it. He said the farther you get from the cities, the less people care what you look like and the more they care whether you will help haul the net. He always helped haul the net.

The sea is full of men who did not fit anywhere else, and he found them in every port. There were smugglers and pearl divers. There were deserters from navies nobody remembered. There were card players who had been asked to leave better towns, and beachcombers who had stopped counting the years. Respectable people called them ne'er-do-wells, and most of them would have agreed. Nathaniel liked them. They did not ask many questions. They kept their word more often than you would think. They could tell a story better than any preacher or professor he ever met.

One of them was a one-eyed ship's cook named Duarte who sailed out of Lourenço Marques. Duarte taught him to play cards and to cook a fish properly and to know when a man was bluffing. Nathaniel said the last was the only one he ever learned all the way, and it was worth more than the other two together. Duarte drank too much and laughed too loud and he was the best friend Nathaniel had in those years. They wrote to each other for forty years. When the letters stopped, Nathaniel knew what it meant, and he went down to the water that night and stood there a long time.

It was in those years that time began to take different shapes. A season on an island where nothing changed could feel like ten years. Ten years in the open water could go by like one long afternoon. He met men when they were young and found them again when they were grandfathers. Sometimes he found their grandchildren, and they told him stories about a strange turtle who had come ashore once and stayed for the harvest. He came to see that time is not a road. It is a current. It moves everyone, but not at the same speed, and not always in the same direction.

Key West

In the early 1920s the Gulf Stream brought him north out of the Caribbean, and he came ashore at Key West.

It was a small town then and a poor one, at the end of everything. There was the white coral rock and the green water and the heat. There were the sponge boats and the cigar makers and the men who ran rum in from Cuba at night with their lights off. There were the bars on Duval Street with the doors open to the street and the ceiling fans turning slowly and not doing any good. Nobody there asked where you came from. It was not that kind of town.

He found work on a charter boat that belonged to a Conch captain named Lee Pinder. Pinder was a big quiet man with burned forearms and pale eyes, and he had been on the water since he was six. He did not talk much and he did not like people who did. He liked Nathaniel right away.

They went out every morning before light. They went out past the reef to where the water turned dark blue and deep, where the Stream ran, and they fished for sailfish and kingfish and, when they were lucky, for marlin. Pinder taught him how to rig a bait and how to read the birds and how to see where the fish were working by the way the water moved. Nathaniel already knew the water. He learned the fishing fast.

There was one fish he talked about for the rest of his life. It was a big blue marlin and it hit in the afternoon, when the sun was low and the sea was heavy. It went off jumping and it jumped eleven times, and then it went down deep and stayed there. They fought it for six hours. Pinder's hands were cut and bleeding from the line, and Nathaniel's shoulders burned, and the boat rolled in the swells and neither of them said anything for a long time. When they brought it alongside at last it was dark and the fish was beautiful and tired and so were they. Pinder looked at it a long time. Then he cut the leader and let it go.

Nathaniel asked him why.

"He fought fair," Pinder said. "So did we. That's enough."

Nathaniel thought about that for fifty years. He said later that everything he ever learned about how a man should behave he learned that night in the boat. You do the work well. You do it honestly. You do not need to own the thing to have done it right.

He spent his evenings in the bars on Duval Street. He drank rum there, and then whiskey, and he sat with the fishermen and the rumrunners and the writers and painters who were beginning to find the island. He listened to them argue about books and fish and the war, and about whether a man could write a true sentence and what it cost him to do it. He did not say much. But he listened, and he remembered, and later he would say that it was in Key West that he first thought he might want to write something down himself. Not yet. But someday.

He stayed two years. Then one morning he told Pinder he was going west. Pinder shook his hand and said, "Water's the other way." Nathaniel said he knew. He said he wanted to see what he was like without it.

Santa Fe

He went a long way west, and in the middle of the 1920s he came to Santa Fe, New Mexico, which is about as far from any ocean as a turtle can go.

Santa Fe was old and new at once. It was full of artists and drifters, ranchers and politicians, people who had come there to start over and people who had come there to hide. The air was thin and dry and smelled of piñon smoke, and the light was hard and clean. A man with a strange face and a strange past did not stand out as much there as you would think.

He found work as a newspaperman, and soon he was a beat reporter covering the police. It was not a glamorous job and he did not want one. He wanted to be where things happened, and the police beat is where things happen. He kept long hours. He knew every desk sergeant and every night clerk and every bail bondsman, and he knew most of the people they dealt with. He wrote clean, short sentences. He did not use a big word when a small one would do, and he did not use two words when one would. The editors complained that he filed late. The readers saw that he was almost never wrong.

He was a man about town. He was in the bars and the restaurants, and he was in the restaurant bars most of all. He said they were the best of both, and that anyone who wanted to know what was really going on in a town should sit in one and keep quiet. He had a usual table in more than one place. He drank his coffee black and his whiskey neat. He listened more than he talked, and people told him things. That was the job, and he was good at it. He wore a jacket that had seen better years and a hat that had seen worse. He tipped well and he left late.

Santa Fe then was thick with painters and poets who had come for the light and stayed for the cheap rent and the long evenings of argument. They were the hipsters of their day, with their borrowed serapes and their strong opinions, and they took to him at once. They thought he was very authentic. He thought they were very young. He sat for portraits he never saw finished. He went to readings and did not always stay awake. One season he was the subject of a very long poem, and he did not want to be, and he was polite about it. He liked them. He liked that they were trying to make something, even when it was not good. He said trying to make something was the main thing.

He was just as much at home at the other end of town. The police beat put him with bootleggers and bail jumpers every day. He knew the card sharps and the petty thieves, the men who sold horses that were not theirs and the women who ran games in back rooms. He treated them the way he treated the mayor. He was polite and he paid attention and he had no illusions. They trusted him for it. More than one gave him a tip that became a story. More than one, people said, came to him before going to the police, to ask whether they would be treated fairly. He was not easy and he was not soft. But he was never cruel, and in that part of town that was worth more than money.

He was a loner. He kept a small apartment with almost nothing in it. He walked alone late at night, after the bars closed, under the big stars, and he could sit by himself at his table for hours and be content. But he made friends, and the friends he made he kept.

There was Ruth Calloway, a painter with a bad temper and a good heart, who said she was the only person in New Mexico who could beat him at gin rummy, and she was right. There was Tommy Baca, a bail bondsman who knew everyone worth knowing and a good many who were not, and who kept Nathaniel's stool at the end of his bar for almost fifty years. There was Lou, who ran an all-night diner off the plaza and would not let him pay for the coffee. Many years later someone asked Ruth about him. "He was the most decent person I ever knew," she said. "And he'd hate that I said so."

He stayed a long time. He stayed long enough to see the town grow and change. He stayed long enough for the boys who had fetched his coffee to become the editors who signed his checks. He covered small crimes and large ones, thefts and fights, swindles and fires, and the quiet kind of rot that never makes the front page.

And he saw bad things in Santa Fe. A police reporter always does, and one who lives a long time sees more. He did not talk about the worst of it. When someone pushed him, he said the desert is beautiful because it does not hide anything, and that people, unfortunately, are not the desert. His friends saw that he grew quieter as the years went on. What they had liked as patience began to look like tiredness. He spent more of his evenings alone.

The Trouble

Nobody really knows what happened at the end of his time in Santa Fe.

There was controversy, and there was a great deal of hubbub. Some people say it started with a story he was working on, about men in positions of trust who could not be trusted, and that he got closer to it than anyone wanted him to. Some people say he fell out with the paper, or with the police, or with someone above them both. Others say it had nothing to do with work. They say a turtle who had outlived several generations of his colleagues was bound to draw the wrong kind of attention sooner or later. The newspapers of the time do not help. The few articles about it disagree with one another, and several of the issues that matter most are missing from the archives.

What is certain is the end. Sometime in the 1970s, Nathaniel Greyback disappeared. He did not file a last story. He did not say goodbye, or if he did, the people he said it to kept it to themselves. His tables were empty. According to one account, his apartment was found clean and bare, with a single coffee cup washed and turned upside down on the drainboard. The people who knew him took it as a message. They never agreed on what it meant.

There were rumors. He had been run out of town. He had been paid to leave. He had gone back to the sea. He had never existed at all and was only a story the old reporters told the young ones. Like most rumors, they said more about the people telling them than about him.

His friends did not believe any of them. Tommy Baca kept the stool at the end of the bar empty for years and would not let anyone sit there. Ruth told anyone who asked that Nathaniel had done nothing wrong, and that whatever had happened, he had gone to protect somebody. She never said who. Lou kept a coffee cup on a hook behind the counter. When people asked whose it was, he said it belonged to a friend who was running late.

Barcelona

He came back, after a while, in Barcelona.

Santa Fe had been as far from the sea as he could go. Barcelona was a kind of coming home. It was a city on the water, crowded and loud and old, with the Mediterranean at the bottom of every street. In the mornings he could smell the salt, and he said that was the first time in fifty years he had slept well. He settled in the way he always did, without any fuss. Soon he had his tables again, in the cafés and the bars and, most of all, in the restaurant bars.

He was not a reporter anymore. Whatever he had seen in Santa Fe, he had decided he was done chasing other people's stories. Now he began to write his own. It was what he had thought about in Key West, all those years before, and now it was time. He wrote in the mornings, early, when the light was good and the street was quiet, and he wrote slowly, and he stopped each day while he still knew what came next. He wrote essays and sketches and short pieces. Some were quiet and some were sharp and a few were very funny. He wrote about honesty, and about what happens when it is gone. He wrote about towns and what holds them together. He wrote about the long view that comes from a long life, and about the small choices that turn out to matter most. He tried to write one true sentence, and then another, and he said that was all there was to it, and that it was very hard.

People who found his work said it read as if it had been written very slowly by someone who had all the time in the world. That was true, and it was the point.

He became, in a small way, known. In some neighborhoods and some cafés he was the writer, the old one, the one with the coffee and the cigarette who would talk to you if you were patient and say nothing at all if you were not. He published in local magazines and small journals, the kind that come and go with their editors. He was never famous. He did not want to be.

The city changed around him the way Santa Fe had. The old neighborhoods filled with young people in vintage coats and heavy glasses, who rode bicycles without gears and argued about records and coffee and opened small cafés in old warehouses. The hipsters found him again, as they always did. They made him a kind of patron saint. They admired his knit cap, which they thought was ironic. It was not. They admired his jacket, which they thought was vintage. It was only old. They asked him to read at their gatherings and to sign magazines that had folded before they were born. He was amused by them and fond of them and sometimes he did not understand them at all. He told one girl he had been wearing that cap since before her grandparents were born. She said that was exactly why it was so great.

And he kept company, as always, with the ne'er-do-wells. He knew the men who worked the docks and the men who worked around the docks. He knew the street musicians, and the pickpockets of the Ramblas, who left him alone, and the old anarchists who met in the back of a bar in the Raval to argue about a war that had ended long ago. He listened to all of them. He said they were the ones who knew what a city really was.

He was still a loner. He lived alone in a small flat with a view of nothing much and a great deal of light. He spent his mornings writing by himself, and it was the best part of the day. But he made friends here too. There was Marta, who ran a secondhand bookshop and kept a shelf of the magazines he wrote for, and who hid the best copies for him. There was Jordi, who tended bar at his café, and poured his coffee before he sat down, and always seemed to know when to leave him alone and when not to. People in that neighborhood still talk about him warmly. They say he remembered everyone's name. They say he never raised his voice. They say he gave away more money than he had, and that if you were in trouble he would sit with you until it passed. "He was slow," Jordi said once. "But he always arrived."

What Remains

Most of what Nathaniel Greyback wrote is lost now. The magazines folded. The journals went out of print. The copies that were left went to attics and secondhand shops and, most of them, to the trash. He kept no archive. He thought of his work the way he thought of his years on the ocean. It had happened. It had been worth doing. It did not need to be held onto.

What he did hold onto was people. For a man who spent so much of his life alone, he left behind a great many people who remember him well. There is an old cook's letters in a shoebox in Mozambique. There is a story told on a charter dock in Key West about a turtle who fought a marlin for six hours and helped let it go. There is a stool at the end of a bar in New Mexico. There is a shelf in a bookshop in Barcelona. Ask anyone who knew him and they say almost the same thing. He listened. He was kind. He told the truth. He was a loner who was never really alone.

But a few things remain. Some of his essays have survived, saved from old issues and loose pages, and they still hold up. They are collected here, with what photographs could be found, so that a new reader might come to know him a little.

Do not read them looking for the whole story. He never told the whole story to anyone. Read them the way he read the water. Read them slowly, and pay attention, and wait to see which way they pull.
